**3. What I Rejected from AI (and Why - The Core of This Assignment):**

- **Rejected AI-Generated Arduino Code (My Personal Case Study):** 
  For my portfolio project, I designed a smart necklace and used an Arduino module to control its light effects. I asked ChatGPT to write the code. The AI gave me a syntactically correct piece of code, but **it completely failed to run**. I rejected it immediately. Why? Because I did not understand the underlying programming logic, I initially could not find the bug. I had to manually debug the code line by line and discovered an error in the lighting loop logic. This rejection taught me that AI cannot replace logical understanding—if I don't know how to code, I cannot even fix AI's mistakes.

- **Rejected AI's Generic Macro Opening:**
  AI initially suggested an opening like "In the 21st century, AI is rapidly changing the world." I rejected this completely. Why? Because the instructor explicitly warned against "macroscopic" and "generic" writing (the "AI is rapidly changing the world" cliché). I replaced it with my own real Arduino failure story, which is exactly the "practice-based case study" the assignment requires.

- **Rejected AI-Recommended Fake References (Critical Verification):**
  AI suggested a highly formatted academic paper with a convincing title and DOI. I rejected it after searching Google Scholar and confirming it was a "hallucinated" reference—it did not exist. Instead, I replaced it with verified, classic sources: Wing (2006) on computational thinking and Resnick et al. (2009) on digital fluency. Both are real, peer-reviewed papers. - **Rejected AI's Flowery but Empty Conclusion:**
  AI wrote a beautiful but generic conclusion. I rejected it and rewrote it to state my core argument: "Generative AI lowers the barrier to writing code, but raises the requirement for designers to verify, critique, and direct computational outputs." This is my own conclusion derived from my debugging experience.
